The Constitution of India, adopted 26 November 1949 and effective 26 January 1950, is the world's longest written constitution. It establishes India as a Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic with a parliamentary system.

Originally 395 articles and 8 schedules — now ~470 articles and 12 schedules after amendments. It draws from multiple sources:

SourceWhat India took
United StatesFundamental Rights, judicial review, independence of judiciary
United KingdomParliamentary system, rule of law, legislative procedure
IrelandDirective Principles of State Policy
CanadaFederal structure with a strong centre
GermanyEmergency provisions, suspension of rights
AustraliaConcurrent List, freedom of trade
South AfricaAmendment procedure

Power is divided between Union and States. Fundamental Rights (Part III) are enforceable in court. Directive Principles (Part IV) are aspirational — they guide policy but can't be sued on. Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, added 1976) are non-enforceable obligations of citizens.

The five that matter most in daily life

Article 21

Right to Life & Personal Liberty

The most powerful article. No person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law — and that procedure must be fair, not merely existent.

Courts have expanded it massively. It now includes:

  • Right to privacy
  • Right to dignity
  • Right to livelihood
  • Right to health and emergency medical care
  • Right to clean environment
  • Right to speedy trial
  • Right to shelter

When it applies Police brutality, illegal detention, hospital refusing emergency treatment, privacy violations, data misuse, demolitions without notice.

Limitation Enforceable against the State (Art. 12), not directly against private parties.

Key case K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) — privacy declared a fundamental right under Art. 21.

Article 22

Protection on Arrest

If arrested, police must tell you why. You must get a lawyer of your choice. You must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours. If any of this doesn't happen — it is illegal detention, challengeable immediately in the High Court via habeas corpus.

Remember The 24-hour clock excludes travel time to the magistrate. But 36 hours is still a violation.

Article 19

Six Freedoms

19(1)(a) — Speech & expression. Say, write, post what you want. Restrictions only for: national security, public order, defamation, decency, foreign relations, contempt of court. Vague "hurting sentiments" is not valid.

19(1)(b) — Peaceful assembly. Protest, gather, demonstrate — must be peaceful and unarmed. Can be regulated (time, place) but not blanket banned.

19(1)(c) — Form associations. Start unions, political parties, NGOs.

19(1)(d) — Move freely across India. No internal passport. No state can stop you at its border.

19(1)(e) — Reside anywhere in India.

19(1)(g) — Practice any profession. State can regulate (licensing) but not arbitrarily ban your livelihood.

Key case S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram (1989) — the heckler's veto. State must protect lawful speech, not shut it down because someone threatens violence.

Article 14

Equality Before Law

Every person is equal before the law. The state cannot treat you arbitrarily — any government action affecting you must have a rational basis. If a government office, bank, or authority singles you out or acts without reason, this is your ground.

The test Is the classification based on an intelligible differentia with a rational nexus to the object of the law?

Article 32

Right to Constitutional Remedies

Ambedkar called it the heart and soul of the Constitution. Without this, rights are words on paper. You can approach the Supreme Court directly if any fundamental right is violated. The High Court equivalent is Art. 226 — and it's broader.

All six categories

Articles 14–18

Right to Equality

Art. 14 — Equality before law. Art. 15 — No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Reservations for SC/ST/OBC and women are permitted exceptions (Art. 15(3), 15(4), 15(5)). Art. 16 — Equal opportunity in government jobs. Art. 17 — Abolition of untouchability, no exceptions, criminal offence. Art. 18 — No titles (Sir, Lord, etc).

Articles 19–22

Right to Freedom

The six freedoms under Art. 19 (detailed above). Art. 20 — Protection if accused: no retrospective criminal law (ex post facto), no double jeopardy, no compelled self-incrimination. Art. 21 — Life and liberty (detailed above). Art. 21A — Right to education for children 6–14. Art. 22 — Protection on arrest (detailed above).

Articles 23–24

Right Against Exploitation

Art. 23 — No forced labour, bonded labour, or human trafficking. Below-minimum-wage work raises a presumption of force. Art. 24 — No child labour (under 14) in factories, mines, or hazardous work. Absolute — no consent can cure it.

Key case Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)

Articles 25–28

Right to Religion

Art. 25 — Freedom of conscience and to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality and health. Art. 26 — Religious denominations may manage their own affairs. Art. 27 — No tax to promote any religion. Art. 28 — No religious instruction in state-funded institutions.

Articles 29–30

Cultural & Educational Rights

Art. 29 — Any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture may conserve it. Art. 30 — Religious and linguistic minorities may establish and administer their own institutions. The state may regulate standards but cannot take over administration.

Article 32

Right to Constitutional Remedies

The enforcement right. Without it, everything above is advisory. The Supreme Court can issue writs: habeas corpus (produce the person), mandamus (do your duty), prohibition (stop exceeding jurisdiction), certiorari (quash an order), quo warranto (by what authority do you hold this office).

Formerly Article 31

Right to Property — removed

Originally a fundamental right. Removed by the 44th Amendment (1978) and made a legal right under Art. 300A. You can still challenge property seizure — just not as a fundamental right.

Federalism — asymmetric, Centre-heavy

The 7th Schedule divides legislative power into three lists:

ListWho legislatesExamples
Union ListCentre onlyDefence, foreign affairs, banking, atomic energy
State ListStates onlyPolice, agriculture, land, public health
Concurrent ListBoth (Centre prevails on conflict)Education, forests, criminal law, marriage

The Centre dominates — it can impose President's Rule (Art. 356), create or alter states, and override in emergencies.

How to amend (Art. 368)

Three routes, depending on what is being changed:

Simple Majority

Both Houses, normal voting. For: admission of new states, creating/abolishing legislatures, citizenship rules. Not formally called an "amendment".

Special Majority

2/3 of members present and majority of total strength, in both Houses. For: most constitutional amendments — fundamental rights, DPSP, elections.

Special Majority + State Ratification

Special majority plus ratification by ≥50% of state legislatures. For: federal structure changes — 7th Schedule, Supreme Court composition, representation of states, Art. 368 itself.

The hard limit: Basic Structure

Parliament can amend the Constitution but cannot destroy its core. The Supreme Court will strike down any amendment that violates the Basic Structure.

Origin Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) — the 13-judge bench that changed Indian constitutional law permanently.

The court has never given an exhaustive list — it's deliberately open-ended. But cases have identified these as part of the core:

  • Supremacy of the Constitution
  • Republican and democratic form of government
  • Secular character
  • Separation of powers
  • Federal character
  • Judicial review
  • Rule of law
  • Free and fair elections
  • Independence of the judiciary
  • Fundamental Rights (especially Art. 32)
  • Unity and integrity of India
  • Parliamentary system

New elements get added case by case. That's the point — it's a living doctrine, not a fixed checklist.

Also see Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) — confirmed and strengthened the doctrine.

If your rights are violated

Step 1: High Court (Art. 226)

Faster, closer, broader jurisdiction than the Supreme Court. For everyday violations — police misconduct, arbitrary detention, government harassment — this is the real tool. You file a writ petition.

Five types of writs: habeas corpus (produce the person), mandamus (do your duty), prohibition (stop), certiorari (quash), quo warranto (by what authority).

Step 2: If you can't afford a lawyer

You are entitled to free legal aid under Art. 39A and the Legal Services Authorities Act. Every district has a DLSA (District Legal Services Authority). National helpline: 15100.

Eligibility SC/ST members, women, children, persons with disabilities, industrial workers, victims of trafficking, persons in custody, those below income threshold — all eligible.

Step 3: Supreme Court (Art. 32)

Only for significant constitutional questions, or when the HC has failed you. Art. 32 lets you approach the SC directly for fundamental rights violations — but practically, the HC is the first stop.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

Any citizen can approach the court on behalf of those who can't access it themselves. This is India's workaround for the access gap. Even a postcard to the SC has historically been treated as a petition (though today a proper filing is practically needed).

Pioneer Justice P.N. Bhagwati, 1970s–80s

E-filing

Available for both SC and most High Courts. Supreme Court: sci.gov.in. You still realistically need a lawyer to draft the petition — filing yourself is allowed but rare.

The honest gap

Every right in Part III exists on paper. Whether it works for you depends on: can you afford a lawyer (or find the DLSA), do you know the right exists, can you take time off for hearings, how far is the court, and will the case conclude in your lifetime — India has 50 million+ cases backlogged.

The law is powerful. The access problem is real. Both are true at the same time.
