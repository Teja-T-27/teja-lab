A Field Guide

Boxing Basics

Most beginners think boxing is about punching hard. It isn't. It's about balance, distance, and timing. Four things make up the foundation: stance, footwork, punches, defense. Everything else sits on top of these.

A way to remember it
Treat them like learning a language. Stance is posture, footwork is grammar, punches are vocabulary, defense is listening. A big vocabulary with broken grammar still sounds off.

02 · StanceYour platform

A stance is a tripod. Two feet, plus your center of gravity between them. When all three are stable, you can punch, move, or absorb a hit without falling. When any one of them is off, you're a step away from the canvas.

The four checks every coach repeats

1 Feet shoulder-width, lead foot forward, rear foot around 45 degrees.

2 Knees soft, never locked. Locked knees mean no balance.

3 Hands high. Lead glove at the cheekbone, rear glove at the jaw.

4 Chin down. Look at the chest; peripheral vision will catch the punches.

Why stand bladed instead of square?
A square stance gives the opponent your whole chest to aim at. Turning sideways cuts the target roughly in half, and it also loads up the back hand so a cross has farther to travel and more rotation behind it.

03 · FootworkMoving without losing your base

Position matters more than power. A fighter who's in the right spot can land a soft jab; a stronger fighter in the wrong spot lands nothing. The rule for all movement is the same: the foot closest to where you want to go moves first, and the other follows to reset the stance. Feet never cross.

How it feels
Good footwork is more like a shuffle than a walk. Short steps, feet close to the floor, weight even. The moment both feet are in the air at the same time, or one crosses the other, a hook can land and you have no way to brace against it.

04 · The Six PunchesSix punches, three shapes

Boxing has six punches. Every combination you see in a professional fight is some arrangement of these. Coaches number them so they can shout combos during a round. "One-two-three" means jab, cross, lead hook.

1 · Jab

Measuring stick

Fast, cheap, low risk. Used to find the range, interrupt the opponent, and set up heavier punches.

2 · Cross

Main power

Rear hand, with the whole body rotating behind it. Power starts at the back foot.

3 · Lead hook

From the side

Horizontal arc to the head or ribs. Comes in on a line the guard isn't covering.

4 · Rear hook

Slow and heavy

A lot of power but a long way to travel. Usually thrown only when the opponent is already hurt.

5 / 6 · Uppercuts

From below

Travels up through the middle. Works best against a bent-over opponent or one with dropped hands.

Reading combos

Just numbers

"1-2" is jab, cross. "1-1-2" is two jabs then a cross. "1-2-3" adds a lead hook on the end.

Power doesn't come from the arm. It comes from the ground, through the legs and hips, and the fist just happens to be where the chain ends.

05 · DefenseFour responses to an incoming punch

When a punch is on its way, there are four things you can do. The first two keep you safer but tire you out; the last two put you closer to a counter but need more skill to pull off.

"You can't hit what isn't there." — Willie Pep. The idea is to make the opponent commit to a punch that lands on empty space.

06 · DistanceWhy range matters more than strength

The thing that decides most exchanges is distance, not strength or speed. Each punch has a range where it lands clean, and ranges where it misses or has no weight behind it. A lot of a fight is spent arguing about which range both fighters are going to be in.

A way to picture it
Range works a bit like gears on a bicycle. On the outside you cover ground easily but can't push much force. On the inside you can hit very hard, but one small balance mistake and you're off your feet. Good boxers switch gears on purpose instead of getting stuck in one.

07 · Putting it togetherThe 1-2

The jab-cross is the first combination any beginner learns. It takes about a second to throw, and all four basics are in it.

Underneath every move is the same question: can I touch you without you touching me?

A guide only goes so far. Pick one basic at a time, work it on a bag or in a mirror, and the rest starts to make sense.
